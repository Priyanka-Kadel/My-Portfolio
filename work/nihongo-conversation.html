Nihongo Nepal: Conversation Practice with an AI Partner

Conversation (कुराकानी अभ्यास, “conversation practice”) is an AI role-play partner inside Nihongo Nepal, a JLPT and JFT exam-prep app. Learners step into a realistic scene and speak Japanese out loud with an AI character. I designed the experience end to end, through to implementation QA. That covered the scenario structure, conversation screen, voice input states, hint system and bilingual message design.

The challenge

What was broken. Nihongo Nepal’s lessons, quizzes and mock tests built reading and listening skills, but nothing in the app let learners practise speaking.

The problem. Learners knew Japanese words and grammar but had nowhere low-pressure to use them in a real conversation, so speaking stayed their weakest skill.

Who it affected

Learners heading to Japan for work or study, who need to speak as well as read. Most of them:

  • have no practice partner, because classmates share the same mistakes and native speakers are rare in Nepal;
  • freeze when they don’t know what to say, especially those with low confidence;
  • study on commutes and in short sessions;
  • read Nepali faster than Japanese.

Why it mattered

Memorised phrases don’t transfer to a real moment, like knocking on a neighbour’s door. Without speaking practice, learners arrive in Japan able to pass a test but not to hold a conversation. Research into speaking anxiety and language learning, checked against Nihongo Nepal’s behavioural persona dimensions, showed what the feature needed:

  • Speaking anxiety is the main barrier. Help had to be one tap away, but hidden until asked for.
  • Context makes language stick. Every conversation needed a concrete scene and goal.
  • Language level matters. Instructions and translations should be in Nepali, with Japanese only where it’s being practised.
  • Open-ended AI chat overwhelms beginners. Scripted goals keep it achievable.
  • Short sessions call for short scenarios. Each scenario had to fit a few minutes, with voice-first input.

What changed

The flow became scene, listen, respond, get help if needed, continue.

Context before conversation.

Before any Japanese appears, learners see an illustration and a Nepali brief. In one scenario, it’s evening and you’re moving into a new room. Your neighbour Lakshmi comes over to help, and you introduce yourselves: your name, that you’re Nepali, and that you’re a student too.

  • The brief doubles as a checklist. Its three goals tell learners exactly what to say.
  • The illustration sets the tone. A friendly scene signals the register without any grammar explanation.
  • The brief stays pinned. It remains visible during the chat, so learners can glance back at their goals.

Japanese first, Nepali as support.

Each AI message shows the character’s name in katakana, the line in Japanese, a speaker icon to replay the audio, and a lighter Nepali translation underneath. The Japanese is large and primary; the translation is there when needed but never competes with it.

Hints on demand, at two levels.

A hint link (संकेत हेर्नुहोस्, “see hint”) sits under the AI’s message.

  • Hidden by default. Learners try first and reach for help only when stuck.
  • Two levels of answer. Tapping the link reveals a full sentence (わたしは ラムです, “I am Ram”) and a short, natural form (ラムです), each with audio. Together they teach that real Japanese drops what’s already understood.
  • Visually distinct. Hint cards are yellow, not blue, so they’re never mistaken for something that was actually said.

Voice input with unmistakable states.

  1. Idle: a large blue microphone button.
  2. Listening: the button turns red with a glow, and “listening…” (सुनिँदै छ…) appears above it.
  3. After speaking: the learner’s words appear in Japanese, so they can check themselves before the AI replies.

AI and voice edge cases considered.

Mismatched script
Speech recognition can return kanji (私) when beginners know the hiragana (わたし).
More than one right answer
The AI must accept answers that differ from the hint, such as the learner’s own name in place of “Ram”.
Silence
It needs a gentle nudge, not an error.
Noise and accents
Commuters practise in noisy places, and Nepali-accented Japanese may be misheard.
Staying on script
The scenario’s goals keep the AI within a beginner’s level.
Response delay
Waiting breaks the feel of a real conversation.
Microphone permission
The app must explain why it needs microphone access.

What improved

  • Learners finally have someone to speak with. The AI gives them a safe, low-pressure place to speak Japanese out loud before they need to in real life.
  • Fear of freezing is lower. Hints are always one tap away, but learners still try first.
  • Practice now has context. Learners rehearse real moments with clear goals, rather than reciting isolated phrases.
  • Pronunciation is easier to learn. Audio plays on every line and hint, as often as the learner wants.
  • There’s no doubt about what the app is doing. Clear voice states show whether it is listening, thinking or waiting for the learner.

What I learned

With AI, structure is the safety net: a clear scene and clear goals made an open-ended AI feel safe for beginners. Help should wait to be asked. And voice needs louder feedback than text.
