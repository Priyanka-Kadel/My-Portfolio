Watobu: Moodboarding for Visual Product Research

Moodboarding lets Watobu users gather real product screens and flows onto a visual board, arrange them, and return to them as their research evolves. As a core contributor, I worked across research, workflow mapping, interaction design, UI, specifications and design QA.

The challenge

What was broken. Watobu helped people collect references, but not make sense of them.

  • Collecting isn’t understanding. Collections stored screens, but didn’t help users group, compare or arrange them.
  • The work left Watobu. Real synthesis happened in Figma or slides, cutting the link back to the source flows.
  • Research went stale. Boards built elsewhere were hard to revisit or update as a project’s needs changed.

The problem. Users had nowhere in Watobu to organise references into a visual argument. Synthesis happened elsewhere and lost its connection to the source.

Who it affected

Designers, product managers and researchers who use references to build an argument rather than one screen at a time. Typical cases:

  • A designer gathering onboarding flows from five competitors to present at a kickoff.
  • A product manager keeping a board open for weeks, adding references as new questions come up.
  • A researcher sharing a board so the whole team debates the same evidence.

Why it mattered

Research becomes useful only once it’s arranged: grouped by pattern, ordered by flow, connected to a takeaway. A flat list of saved screens holds evidence but not insight. Within Watobu, search finds references and bookmarks keep them, but the step where references turn into a decision was missing.

Research into moodboarding and visual-organisation tools, and into products in my reference library, showed three patterns:

Canvas or grid
Free canvases give freedom but get messy fast; grids stay clean but can feel rigid.
Add in context
Adding works best from wherever something is found, not only from inside the board.
Clusters over positions
People think in clusters, like “onboarding” or “empty states”, rather than precise positions.

What changed

Moodboarding gave Watobu a workflow of collect, organise, revisit, share. It is now the step after search and bookmarking, where references become a point of view. The design was shaped by five challenges:

  1. Effortless board creation. A setup step slows people down, but skipping it leaves boards unnamed and hard to find. Creating a board had to feel like a natural part of saving a reference.
  2. Flexible but tidy. The workspace needed to stay clear by default while still letting people rearrange.
  3. Clarity with many references. Dense SaaS screenshots blur together at small sizes. Every reference needed consistent presentation, plus enough context (which app, which flow) to stay recognisable.
  4. Safe adding and removing. Removing an item had to be easy but never accidental.
  5. Built for ongoing research. Boards are revisited over weeks, so getting back to recent work had to be quick.

What improved

  • Synthesis stays in Watobu. Users can organise references where they found them, instead of moving the thinking into another tool.
  • References keep their source. A board stays connected to the original screens and flows.
  • Research stays alive. Boards can be revisited and updated as a project moves on, rather than going stale in a slide deck.
  • Teams share one body of evidence. A shared board lets everyone debate the same references.

What I learned

Visual research tools succeed when they help people think, not just store. Organising content is a design problem in its own right. Where things go and how they’re grouped shapes what people notice, compare and conclude.
